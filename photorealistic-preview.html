Sinking Sim — Photorealistic Graphics Preview

A visual proposal for what a "much more photo-realistic" version of Sinking Sim would look like. The current build favors legibility and physics focus (flat-ish water, simple solid-color hulls, blocky geometry). This document shows the target look — what we're aiming at — using SVG concept art so you can see the difference before any code is written.

The goal isn't Hollywood VFX; it's "feels like a real ship on a real ocean at a real time of day" using techniques Three.js handles well: PBR materials, reflective water, HDR-ish sky, normal-mapped surfaces, baked ship paint, instanced foam and spray, volumetric haze.

1. Hero shot — RMS Titanic at golden hour

The single image that defines the new aesthetic. Calm Atlantic evening, sun low on the horizon, ship lit from the side with long shadows, ocean reflecting both sky and hull.

Key elements: sky gradient with sun disc + glow, reflective ocean with specular streaks and a broken sun-path, ship silhouette with antifouling red below waterline, four buff funnels, lifeboats and lit portholes, foam at the bow, subtle reflection.

2. Ocean — current vs. photorealistic

The biggest visible jump. Current water is a single shaded plane with sine-wave displacement and a flat color. Target water is a PBR surface with normal mapping, sky reflection, specular highlights, foam at crests and around the hull, and a deeper dark zone below.

3. Hull detail — close-up

Right now the ship is a solid-color hull with a thin red strip below the waterline. Photorealistic hulls have: visible plating seams, rivet rows, portholes, anchors, draft markings, weathering streaks, and the subtle gloss of wet paint near the waterline.

How this maps to Three.js: normal map for plating seams (procedural or a small baked texture), instanced rivet geometry or normal-map detail, real porthole geometry (cylinder + inset glass with emissive map for lit interiors), painted draft numerals, vertex-colored weathering streaks, screen- space wetness near the dynamic waterline.

4. The fleet — each ship distinct

Right now every ship is the same builder with different parameters, so they read as "Titanic, but shorter / longer / different funnel count." The photorealistic pass gives each one its own recognizable silhouette and

distinct deck arrangement so picking from the list actually feels different.

5. Disaster rendering — iceberg strike

The current "disasters" mostly differ by label. The photorealistic pass makes each disaster look distinct: an iceberg is a blue-white mass of ice with turquoise water staining around it; a tsunami is a curling green-blue wall; a mine strike is an underwater flash and bubble plume; a boiler explosion is venting smoke and a visible hull breach.

6. Atmosphere presets

A photorealistic renderer wants time-of-day and weather variety. Picker options, not just one "look":

Preset parameters that change: sun direction + color, sky gradient, fog density and color, water reflectance, tone-mapping exposure, shadow softness, particle count for rain/spray, exposure compensation.

Total is a substantial graphics pass but mostly additive — the physics, ship picker, disaster selection, and HUD all stay the same.

8. What this is not

  • Not Hollywood VFX. We won't ship Arnold-rendered ocean displacements or

hand-painted textures from concept artists.

  • Not a perf budget blowout. Everything above targets a single 1080p

desktop browser at 30–60 fps on integrated GPU. Worst-case is the Gerstner wave density and the env-map resolution, both tunable.

  • Not a rewrite. The current physics.js, disasters.js, ships.js data,

and main.js loop stay; this is a render-layer upgrade on top.

9. Next step

  2. Then #2 + #3 (Gerstner waves + foam) — the ocean becomes the actual star.
  3. Then #4 + #5 (hull detail + per-ship liveries) — ships stop feeling like

reskins.
